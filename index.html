Across the reproductive life cycle

First Period, Last Period & Beyond

Menstruation changes across life. Understanding the first period, pregnancy, postpartum changes and menopause can make these transitions less confusing.

01 • FIRST PERIOD

How does the first period happen?

Menarche is the first menstrual period. During puberty, hormone signals from the brain and ovaries coordinate changes in the reproductive system. The uterine lining thickens as part of the cycle; after ovulation, if pregnancy does not occur, the lining is shed through the vagina as menstrual bleeding.

What can it feel like? The first period may be light or moderate and may last a few days. Early cycles can be irregular while the body’s hormone system matures.

Prepare: Keep a menstrual product available, learn how to use it, track the first few cycles, and talk to a trusted adult or healthcare professional if something feels unusual or worrying.

02 • CYCLE & OVULATION

What happens between periods?

The cycle includes menstruation, the follicular phase, ovulation and the luteal phase. Hormones help an ovarian follicle mature and, around ovulation, an egg may be released. The uterine lining also changes in preparation for a possible pregnancy.

Remember: Cycle length varies, especially in the first years after the first period. Tracking dates, bleeding and symptoms can help you notice your own pattern.

03 • PREGNANCY & CONCEPTION

How can conception happen?

Pregnancy can occur when sperm fertilizes an egg, usually around the time of ovulation. Fertilization generally occurs in a fallopian tube; the resulting embryo then travels toward the uterus and can implant in the uterine lining.

Why periods stop: When pregnancy occurs, the hormonal pattern changes and the uterine lining is maintained rather than shed as a normal menstrual period.

Important: A missed period can have many causes. A pregnancy test and appropriate healthcare advice can help determine the reason.

04 • AFTER DELIVERY

What happens to periods after childbirth?

Menstruation does not necessarily return immediately after delivery. Breastfeeding can delay ovulation and the return of periods, while timing varies greatly between people.

Bleeding after birth is not the same as a normal menstrual period. Postpartum bleeding gradually changes as the uterus heals. Seek urgent medical care for very heavy bleeding, severe pain, fever, fainting, breathing difficulty or other concerning symptoms.

Fertility can return before the first period, so pregnancy is possible even before menstruation resumes.

05 • LAST PERIOD & MENOPAUSE

What is the last period?

The transition leading up to menopause is called perimenopause. Periods may become less predictable, shorter or longer, lighter or heavier. Menopause is confirmed after 12 consecutive months without a menstrual period when there is no other cause.

After menopause: Any new vaginal bleeding should be discussed with a healthcare professional rather than automatically assumed to be a period.

06 • TRACK & UNDERSTAND

What should you track?

Record the first day of bleeding, approximate flow, pain, important symptoms, missed periods and major changes. Tracking is not about judging your body — it gives you useful information to discuss with a healthcare professional.

Get checked for persistent or severe pain, very heavy bleeding, repeated missed periods, bleeding after menopause, or other changes that concern you.

Educational note: These life-stage explanations describe common physiology, not a diagnosis. Timing varies between people. For pregnancy, postpartum or menopause concerns, a qualified healthcare professional can provide individual guidance.

Medical reference

Period Problems & Conditions

Understand the condition, what you may observe, habits and factors that can influence symptoms, what you can start doing now, and when professional assessment matters. These pages explain possibilities — they do not diagnose you.

Common symptom / condition

Dysmenorrhea (painful periods)

What is it? Pain or cramping around menstruation, often caused by uterine contractions. Sometimes painful periods are linked to another condition.

What might you notice? Lower-abdominal cramps, pain spreading to the back or thighs, nausea, headache or tiredness.

Habits & factors: Poor sleep, high stress and low physical activity may make some people's symptoms harder to manage, but these habits do not mean you caused the condition.

Start from now: Track pain and cycle timing; gentle movement, rest, hydration, a warm bath or a safely wrapped heat source may help when comfortable.

Get checked if: pain is severe, getting worse, occurs outside the period, or stops you from normal activities.

Chronic condition

Endometriosis

What is it? Tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus and can cause inflammation and pain.

What might you notice? Severe period pain, heavy bleeding, chronic pelvic pain, fatigue, bloating or nausea; bowel, bladder or fertility-related symptoms can also occur.

Habits & factors: The cause of endometriosis is not fully known and there is no known lifestyle method that prevents it. Do not blame food, exercise or personal habits for causing it.

Start from now: Keep a symptom diary and record pain, bleeding, bowel/bladder symptoms and how they affect school or daily life.

Get checked if: symptoms repeatedly disrupt daily life or pain persists or worsens.

Uterine condition

Adenomyosis

What is it? Tissue similar to the uterine lining grows into the muscular wall of the uterus.

What might you notice? Painful periods, heavy or prolonged bleeding, pelvic pressure or tenderness.

Habits & factors: Adenomyosis is not simply caused by eating certain foods or having a particular lifestyle. The exact cause is not fully understood.

Start from now: Record bleeding duration, product changes, pain level and other symptoms so a clinician can see the pattern.

Get checked if: bleeding or pain is persistent, heavy or affecting daily life.

Bleeding problem

Heavy menstrual bleeding

What is it? Menstrual bleeding that is unusually heavy or prolonged and affects daily life.

What might you notice? Very frequent product changes, bleeding through clothes or bedding, periods lasting more than 7 days, fatigue, dizziness or breathlessness.

Habits & factors: Heavy bleeding can have many causes, including hormonal changes, fibroids or bleeding disorders. Stress or lifestyle changes can affect cycles, but heavy bleeding should not automatically be blamed on habits.

Start from now: Track days of bleeding, approximate product changes and symptoms such as fatigue or dizziness. Seek medical advice if it keeps happening.

Get checked if: heavy bleeding is recurring, disruptive, or associated with significant weakness, dizziness or breathlessness.

Hormonal & metabolic condition

PCOS

What is it? Polycystic ovary syndrome is a hormonal and metabolic condition that can affect ovulation, periods and androgen levels.

What might you notice? Irregular or infrequent periods, acne, increased facial/body hair, scalp hair thinning or difficulty with ovulation can occur. Symptoms vary.

Habits & factors: PCOS is not caused simply by eating junk food. Genetics and hormone/metabolic factors are important. A pattern of highly processed foods, low physical activity, poor sleep and other factors may worsen metabolic health in some people, so healthy everyday habits can be useful alongside medical care. PCOS can occur at any body size.

Start from now: Track cycle patterns, aim for regular balanced meals, adequate sleep and comfortable regular activity, and discuss persistent irregularity with a healthcare professional rather than self-diagnosing.

Get checked if: periods are repeatedly absent/irregular or you notice persistent acne, excess hair growth or other concerning changes.

Cycle pattern

Amenorrhea & irregular periods

What is it? Amenorrhea means periods are absent; irregular periods can have many causes.

What might you notice? Long gaps, unpredictable timing, sudden changes from your usual pattern or repeated missed periods.

Habits & factors: Major stress, not eating enough, rapid weight changes, very intense exercise and some medical conditions or medicines can affect menstrual cycles. Do not intentionally restrict food or over-exercise to control your period.

Start from now: Track cycles, sleep, stress, nutrition and exercise changes, and seek care for persistent changes.

Get checked if: a period stops for more than 3 months without an explanation, or the change is persistent or accompanied by other concerning symptoms.

Premenstrual condition

PMS & PMDD

What is it? PMS involves physical or emotional symptoms before a period; PMDD is a more severe condition that can significantly affect daily life.

What might you notice? Bloating, breast tenderness, headaches, mood changes, irritability, appetite changes or sleep changes. Severity varies.

Habits & factors: Stress, poor sleep and some dietary patterns may worsen symptoms for some people. This does not mean PMS/PMDD is a personal failure or something you caused.

Start from now: Track symptoms across several cycles, keep a consistent sleep routine, stay active in ways that feel comfortable and eat regular balanced meals.

Get checked if: symptoms are severe, repeatedly disrupt school/daily activities, or significantly affect wellbeing.

Other causes

Fibroids, thyroid problems & bleeding disorders

What are they? Fibroids are growths in or around the uterus; thyroid disorders and bleeding disorders can also change menstrual patterns.

What might you notice? Heavy/prolonged bleeding, irregular periods, fatigue, easy bruising or other symptoms depending on the cause.

Habits & factors: These conditions are not simply caused by junk food or poor hygiene. Some thyroid and bleeding problems are medical conditions that need assessment.

Start from now: Keep a clear record of bleeding and associated symptoms and mention relevant family history to a clinician.

Get checked if: bleeding changes substantially, is heavy, or symptoms persist.

Use • Care • Dispose

Menstrual Products & Comfort Tools

Products do different jobs. Learn what each one is for, how to use it safely, how to care for it, and what to do when it reaches the end of its life.

PAD

Disposable sanitary pads

Use: Attach the adhesive side to underwear and choose an absorbency that suits your flow.

Change: Follow the product instructions and change regularly, especially when wet or full.

Dispose: Wrap the used pad and place it in an appropriate waste bin. Do not flush it.

T

Tampons

Use: Insert according to the manufacturer's instructions and choose the lowest absorbency that works for you.

Change: Follow the package instructions and never leave a tampon in longer than recommended.

Dispose: Wrap the used tampon and put it in a waste bin. Do not flush it.

CUP

Menstrual cups

Use: Fold and insert the cup according to its instructions; it collects rather than absorbs menstrual fluid.

Care: Empty, rinse and reinsert as directed. At the end of the cycle, clean/boil it for the time specified by the manufacturer.

Dispose: A reusable cup is not normally thrown away each cycle. Replace it when damaged or at the end of its recommended service life.

PW

Period underwear

Use: Wear like normal underwear and choose the absorbency suited to your flow.

Care: Rinse/wash and dry thoroughly according to the care label.

Dispose: When worn out, follow local textile/waste options where available.

RP

Reusable cloth pads

Use: Secure around underwear with the fastener and change regularly.

Care: Wash thoroughly with soap/detergent and clean water, then dry completely before storing.

Dispose: When damaged beyond reuse, follow local textile/waste guidance.

L

Panty liners

Use: Useful for lighter flow or spotting when appropriate.

Change: Change regularly and whenever damp/uncomfortable.

Dispose: Wrap and place in a bin. Never flush.

HOT

Hot water bottle

Use: Fill with hot—not boiling—water according to the bottle instructions. Close securely and wrap it in a cloth before placing it on the abdomen.

Safety: Use warm heat for a comfortable period, check your skin, and do not sleep with a hot water bottle. Replace it if it becomes cracked, worn or leaks.

After use: Empty it, allow it to dry and store it according to the manufacturer's instructions.

HEAT

Reusable heat pad

Use: Warm it exactly as directed by the manufacturer and place it over clothing or with a protective layer if required.

Safety: Avoid very high heat, prolonged direct contact and sleeping while it is switched on/heated.

Care: Follow the product's cleaning and storage instructions.

BATH

Warm bath or shower

Use: A comfortably warm bath or shower may help some people relax during cramps.

Safety: Avoid water temperatures that are hot enough to burn or make you feel faint.

After: Dry yourself and change into clean, comfortable clothing.

TRACK

Cycle & symptom tracker

Use: Record period dates, bleeding pattern, pain and other symptoms.

Why: A simple record can help you notice changes and communicate clearly with a healthcare professional.

Privacy: Avoid entering sensitive health information into an app you do not trust.

Hygiene & Safety

Small habits can make menstrual management safer and more comfortable.

01

Clean hands

Wash or clean your hands before and after changing menstrual products.

02

Follow instructions

Use each product according to its manufacturer guidance and intended use.

03

Keep reusable products clean

Wash reusable products properly and allow them to dry completely.

04

Dispose responsibly

05

Do not use contaminated, damaged or inappropriate materials as menstrual products.

06

Make the days easier

Period Comfort

There is no single “perfect” period routine. Choose practical options that suit your body and circumstances.

Comfort measures

  • Heat can help some people with cramps.
  • Gentle movement may feel helpful if comfortable.
  • Rest and adequate sleep matter.
  • Hydration and regular meals can support wellbeing.

Nutrition

  • Eat a balanced variety of foods.
  • Include iron-rich foods and protein.
  • Include fruits and vegetables.
  • Avoid rigid “period diets” or extreme restriction.

At school

  • Keep an emergency menstrual product available when possible.
  • Use a clean, private changing space.
  • Talk to a trusted adult if you need support.
  • Severe symptoms should not simply be ignored.

Challenge misinformation

Myth vs Fact

Here, the myth and the fact are shown side by side so the difference is immediately clear.

MYTH
FACT

“Period blood is dirty.”

Menstrual blood is something the body naturally sheds during menstruation.

Menstruation is a normal biological process.

Stigma and shame are not medical requirements. People deserve accurate information and dignity.

“Severe period pain is always normal.”

You should simply tolerate it because periods are supposed to hurt.

Severe or disruptive pain deserves attention.

Very painful periods can sometimes be associated with conditions such as endometriosis or adenomyosis.

“Everyone has a 28-day cycle.”

A cycle that differs from 28 days must be abnormal.

Cycle length varies.

People can have different cycle patterns, and patterns can change over time.

“You cannot exercise during a period.”

All activity should be avoided during menstruation.

Activity can be individual.

Gentle movement may help some people, while rest is appropriate when someone does not feel well.

“A tampon or pad can be flushed.”

Flushing menstrual products is an easy way to dispose of them.

Most menstrual products should go in a waste bin.

Wrap disposable products and place them in appropriate waste. Do not flush pads, liners or tampons.

“One symptom proves you have a disease.”

If you have cramps or irregular periods, you can diagnose yourself from a website.

Symptoms have many possible causes.

Education can help you recognize patterns, but diagnosis requires appropriate professional assessment.

Know when to seek care

When Is It NOT Normal?

Pay attention to

  • Severe or worsening pain
  • Very heavy bleeding
  • Unusually long-lasting bleeding
  • Bleeding between periods
  • Repeatedly missing periods or major cycle changes
  • Symptoms that seriously interfere with school or daily life
  • Significant weakness, dizziness or breathlessness associated with heavy bleeding

Need medical advice?

Ritu Rhythms provides education, not diagnosis. A qualified healthcare professional can assess symptoms, consider possible causes and recommend appropriate care.
